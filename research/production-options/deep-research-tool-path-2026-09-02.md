## 2. The Technical and Legal Design Space for Hypnotic Techno Engines

To reason clearly about tools and architectures, it helps to distinguish the main categories of systems you could build. At a high level, there are at least three broad approaches to generating or assembling hypnotic techno for your app. The first is **fully generative AI models**—either local open-source models like ACE-Step 1.5 or cloud-based models accessed through APIs, such as Stable Audio 2.5, Mubert’s engine, or Google’s Lyria family.[2][6][7][9][13][16] These models take text or other prompts and directly synthesize audio. They vary in the degree to which they can be controlled in terms of tempo, key, genre, and structure, and they differ dramatically in licensing: some are open-sourced under MIT or Apache and allow unrestricted commercial use of outputs, while others are offered as services with tightly constrained rights.

The second category is **hybrid generative–combinatorial systems**, where AI is used primarily as a high-level planner or as a generator of stems or phrases, but the actual long-form output is assembled via deterministic rules, such as layer selection, crossfading, and spatialization. ACE-Step 1.5 explicitly embodies this philosophy: it uses a language model (LM) as a “planner” that turns user prompts into a detailed song blueprint, including metadata and lyrics, and then a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) decoder synthesizes the audio from that blueprint.[6] The architectural separation between planning and decoding allows, in principle, for more controllable structure and the generation of shorter building blocks that can be stitched into longer, continuous flows. Your earlier coded synthesis experiments also fall into this category: a procedural sequencer driving synthesized sound sources, with the option to swap the raw sound layer for stems produced by human sound designers or AI models.

The third category is **non-AI sample or stem-based engines**, which might resemble what Endel does: a library of professionally produced stems in specific keys, tempos, and moods, combined at runtime based on rule sets and user inputs. This approach does not depend on AI at all; instead it leans on human-produced content and deterministic recombination. It has the advantage of easily passing the ear test when the stems come from experienced techno producers and are mixed and mastered properly. However, it shifts your technical and legal challenges into the domains of content acquisition and licensing: you must either commission bespoke stem packs or buy them under licenses that explicitly allow embedding in an app as a runtime library, something many off-the-shelf sample packs do not allow.

Legal considerations intersect differently with each of these categories. Local open-source models such as ACE-Step 1.5, released under the MIT license, typically grant you broad rights to use, modify, and redistribute the software and weights, and model cards can clarify that generated music can be used commercially because the training data is legally compliant and consists of licensed, royalty-free, or public domain material.[2][5][6][10] In this case, your main legal questions are about how you prompt and whether you inadvertently produce content too close to specific copyrighted works. By contrast, cloud APIs such as Stable Audio 2.5 and Mubert’s engine come with service terms that control not only how you call the model but also what you may do with the outputs, often granting royalty-free sync rights for videos, apps, and games while forbidding resale as stock music or use in competing services.[7][13][18][19] You must study these terms closely, because using such services to build an AI-powered focus music app enters a grey zone between normal content creator usage and building a competing audio service.

Consumer-facing AI music platforms such as Suno, Udio, Beatoven, and Soundraw typically fall into yet another legal pattern. Their terms are written for content creators who want to use AI music as background tracks in videos or to release songs, not for developers who want to embed generated audio into an app as a dynamic service. For example, recent analyses of Suno’s commercial terms show that while paid subscribers may be assigned ownership of their outputs, commercial exploitation is only permitted for outputs that have been downloaded via an official channel and in accordance with download allocations for each service tier, with free plans limited to seven lifetime non-commercial downloads and Pro and Premier plans offering capped monthly download counts.[11][12] Suno’s terms also prohibit using outputs to compete with Suno, and explicitly state that you may not commercially exploit outputs obtained by stream ripping or other non-official means.[11][12][20] Udio’s terms go even further in the restrictive direction, stating that Udio and its licensors own all rights in the service and generated works, and that users may only use outputs for personal and non-commercial purposes unless the service explicitly permits otherwise.[14][17] Such terms strongly disfavor using these services as engines for another app’s paid music library.

Beyond legal status, the **musical structure requirements** of hypnotic focus techno must shape your choice of tools. Many general-purpose AI music systems are trained heavily on songs with strong melodic and structural arcs, including pop, rock, and cinematic genres, and they tend to generate verse–chorus-bridge forms with vocals, drops, and builds unless carefully steered. Google’s Lyria 3 Pro model, for instance, is explicitly described as being able to generate multi-minute songs with distinct verses, choruses, and bridges, with prompts controlling key, mood, and duration, and even timestamped event control.[9] While this is powerful, it is the opposite of what you want: you need long spans of subtle evolution with no obvious thematic “events.” By contrast, Lyria RealTime is designed for continuous instrumental generation with real-time control of tempo, density, and brightness, and session lengths of up to ten minutes,[15] which aligns much better with your intended use, provided you can control its harmonic and timbral tendencies to land in the dub techno aesthetic.

Finally, the **practical operational model** matters. Cloud APIs like Stable Audio 2.5 run entirely on the provider’s infrastructure and charge per generation; Stable Audio 2.5, for example, is priced on the Stability Developer Platform at twenty cents per generation regardless of duration, with authentication via API key.[7] At 500 generations per month, that implies around one hundred dollars per month, which is within your budget. Local models like ACE-Step 1.5 run entirely on your Mac mini, requiring careful optimization and possibly some quantization or CPU offloading to fit within 24 GB of memory, but incurring no per-track costs once installed.[2][5][6] Hybrid sample-based engines built on commissioned stems require no heavy compute but depend on upfront or ongoing payments to human producers, as well as storage and bandwidth for the audio assets.

With these dimensions in mind, the next section ranks a shortlist of up to six tools or engines that plausibly fit your use case, and evaluates each with respect to driving method, quality evidence for minimal/dub techno, controllability, output continuity, licensing for in-app use, per-month cost at 500 renders, and setup effort.

### 3.1 ACE-Step 1.5 XL: Local, MIT-Licensed Music Foundation Model

ACE-Step 1.5 XL is the most promising core engine for your app because it combines three critical properties: it is open source under the MIT license, it is architected for efficient local inference on consumer hardware, and it was designed explicitly as a **music foundation model** capable of high-fidelity multi-genre generation that benchmarks above top commercial systems such as Suno v5 on standard music evaluation metrics.[1][2][5][6][10] The XL variant introduces a 4-billion-parameter Diffusion Transformer decoder that requires roughly nine gigabytes of VRAM for weights and is reported to achieve higher audio quality than the smaller 2B model, with recommended VRAM of at least twelve gigabytes with offloading or twenty gigabytes without, something your 24 GB Mac mini can approximate via efficient CPU–GPU memory management.[1][2] Public reports and demos on Hugging Face emphasize that ACE-Step 1.5 can generate coherent, high-fidelity, full-length songs across genres with fast generation times—under ten seconds for a full track on an RTX 3090—and that it features a hybrid LM–DiT architecture optimized for local deployment.[2][6]

Driving ACE-Step 1.5 XL can be done entirely locally through a Gradio-based web user interface or via a Python API, with installation instructions provided in both the GitHub repository and the Hugging Face demo README.[1][2][6] The recommended local installation involves cloning the GitHub repository, installing dependencies using a tool like `uv sync` or `pip install -e .`, and then launching either a Gradio web UI (`uv run acestep`) or a REST API server (`uv run acestep-api`) listening on localhost.[1][2][6] Once running, you can issue text prompts that describe the desired style, mood, and structure, optionally including lyrics that the LM planner uses when constructing its song blueprint, though in your case you would specify instrumental-only prompting. The architecture, in which the LM planner converts simple user queries into detailed song blueprints and feeds these to the DiT decoder, is especially appealing for your task because it naturally supports controlling structural aspects such as section length, density, and arrangement, even if the default prompt templates are song-oriented.[6]

Regarding **quality for minimal/dub techno**, there is limited genre-specific evaluation in the public documentation, but several lines of evidence support its suitability. First, the ACE-Step team reports that on commonly used evaluation metrics, ACE-Step v1.5 achieves quality beyond most commercial music models while remaining extremely fast, and the XL 4B DiT is described as providing “higher audio quality” compared to the 2B variant.[2] A Japanese technical blog that tested ACE-Step 1.5 XL on various genres notes that benchmarks claim it surpasses Suno v5 on all song-level metrics and concludes that ACE-Step 1.5 XL is currently the strongest choice among open-source music generative models with MIT-licensed commercial use rights.[5] While this blog does not focus specifically on dub techno, it notes that ACE-Step’s LM planner plus DiT decoder can capture diverse structures, and their testing found that in many cases the quality was comparable to Suno’s and that the ability to run locally offered significant creative freedom.[5] Given that techno and house are common genres in modern generative music datasets, and that ACE-Step’s training data includes licensed and royalty-free music as well as synthetic MIDI-to-audio data, it is extremely likely that minimal and dub techno styles are within its learned space.[10]

Controllability is a mixed story but likely sufficient for your needs. ACE-Step uses natural language prompts as its primary control mechanism, and while its documentation emphasizes genre, mood, and task controls (such as text-to-music, cover, repaint, and extract), it does not yet expose an explicit BPM parameter at the model level in the way Lyria RealTime does.[2][6][10] However, prompt engineering practices from other systems, such as Suno, suggest that specifying an exact BPM in the textual prompt significantly influences the tempo, especially for techno where tempo is a core stylistic feature.[8] You can therefore craft prompts such as “minimal dub techno, 118 BPM, deep steady four-on-the-floor kick, reverb-drenched chord stabs, tape delay, spacious mix, warm sub bass, hypnotic and atmospheric, instrumental only” to bias the model towards the right tempo and mood, borrowing language from the techno prompt guides used for other models.[8] The LM planner’s ability to generate chain-of-thought style blueprints including structure and internal captions may further help the DiT decoder maintain a consistent groove and texture over longer durations, though careful evaluation is needed.[6]

Regarding **output length and continuity**, ACE-Step can generate full songs, and the LM planner is reported to scale from short loops to ten-minute compositions.[6] This is particularly relevant for your 1–3 hour sessions: although generating continuous three-hour audio is impractical, you can pre-generate or stream-generate a sequence of coherent ten-minute segments and crossfade them in your runtime engine. Because the model supports text-to-music and “lego” or “complete” tasks that allow combining or extending audio segments, there is also potential to stitch together segments with overlapping content to improve continuity.[6][10] Practically, for an MVP, you can treat ACE-Step as a generator of high-quality ten-minute “blocks” that your app sequences with gentle transitions, creating the illusion of infinite flow while still operating within the model’s sweet spot.

Legally, ACE-Step 1.5 XL stands out as one of the few models whose license and training data story are fully aligned with your needs. The project is released under the MIT license, a permissive open-source license that allows use, modification, distribution, and private or commercial deployment of the software and associated weights.[2][6] The XL SFT model card explicitly states that ACE-Step 1.5 was trained on legally compliant datasets, including licensed music, royalty-free or public domain recordings, and synthetic MIDI-to-audio data, and highlights that generated music can be used for commercial purposes.[10] It also describes the model as “commercial-ready” and emphasizes safe training data.[10] A Japanese review reiterates that ACE-Step 1.5 XL is MIT-licensed and commercially usable, making it a prime choice among open models for developers wanting to embed music generation in products.[5] Because you would be running ACE-Step locally, you are not subject to the provider’s service terms beyond the MIT license, and you can treat the generated music as your own works, subject only to general copyright law and any model-specific disclaimers.

In terms of **cost at 500 renders per month**, ACE-Step incurs no per-render pricing since it runs on your own hardware. Your costs are limited to electricity and any opportunity costs of using the Mac mini’s resources, all of which fall well within your budget. Compute performance on an M-series Mac with 24 GB of memory will not match an RTX 3090, but ACE-Step’s base models are optimized for consumer hardware, and the documentation indicates that even XL can be run with memory offload and quantization on hardware with 12 GB of VRAM or equivalent memory.[1][2][6] Even if each ten-minute segment takes several minutes to generate, you can pre-render a catalog of segments offline and then use them in the app, avoiding real-time latency constraints.

Setup effort is moderate but manageable, especially given your comfort with AI coding agents. The GitHub repository provides turn-key scripts for Windows, Linux, and macOS, including a macOS Apple Silicon launcher that sets execute permissions and runs a Gradio UI, downloading models on the first run.[1] The Hugging Face README describes an alternative installation path using the `uv` package manager and a simple `uv run acestep` command to start the UI or API.[6] Integrating ACE-Step into your app’s backend will require writing a thin orchestration layer in Python or another language to call the local API, manage prompts, and store generated files or streams. None of this demands low-level DSP or GPU programming, and skills like prompt design and curation will likely be more critical than coding.

Taken together, ACE-Step 1.5 XL is your **top-ranked** engine because it marries high reported quality, explicit commercial usability, local deployment, and a strong architectural fit for your style of music. It is the single tool most likely to give you an ear-test winner within days, while also underpinning a legally robust, scalable runtime engine.

### 3.2 Google Lyria RealTime and Lyria 3: Streaming and Clip-Based Generative Music

Google’s Lyria family consists of two related offerings: the **Lyria 3** Clip and Pro models accessed via the Gemini API, and the experimental **Lyria RealTime** model accessed via WebSocket for real-time interactive music generation.[9][15] Both produce 44.1 kHz stereo audio from text prompts, with the Pro model capable of full-length songs of a couple of minutes and the Clip model generating fixed 30-second clips.[9] For your specific use case of hypnotic techno, Lyria RealTime is the more interesting of the two, because it is designed for continuous instrumental generation with user control over musical parameters in real time.[15]

Lyria 3 Clip is primarily a content-creation tool: a developer can call the generateContent API with `model="lyria-3-clip-preview"` and a textual description, and the model returns a 30-second clip in the requested style.[9] The Pro model can create multi-minute compositions with clear song structures—verses, choruses, and bridges—and supports specifying the desired length in the prompt or by using timestamps to define the structure.[9] Both Clip and Pro allow additional prompt parameters such as key or scale (for example “in G major” or “D minor”), mood and atmosphere descriptors (“nostalgic,” “aggressive,” “ethereal”), and duration.[9] For techno, these controls allow you to specify tempo ranges (e.g., “create a 2-minute minimal dub techno track at 120 BPM with deep, spacious atmosphere”) and ensure the music stays in an appropriate key and mood. However, because Pro is oriented towards songs with distinct sections, you must take care in prompt engineering to suppress big builds or bridges and emphasize a “loop-like,” “steady,” or “gradual” structure to avoid interruptions to focus.

Lyria RealTime, by contrast, is purpose-built for interactive, continuous music. The RealTime guide describes how developers can connect via bidirectional WebSocket to a `models/lyria-realtime-exp` endpoint and stream text prompts and parameter adjustments while receiving a continuous stream of generated audio.[15] Lyria RealTime supports real-time control over parameters such as tempo, density, and brightness, allowing you to adjust energy and texture live.[15] Sessions are limited to ten minutes and are instrumental-only, which aligns with your need for non-vocal, non-lyrical focus music.[15] The documentation notes that all audio output carries an inaudible digital watermark from Google’s SynthID, and it explicitly advises developers to check license conditions before commercial distribution.[15] It also notes that for a continuous experience beyond ten minutes, developers must implement logic to seamlessly start a new session before the previous one reaches the ten-minute limit and crossfade between sessions.[15]

In terms of **quality for minimal/dub techno**, public demos of Lyria and related models have shown strong results in a wide range of genres, and the control over tempo and density in RealTime should, in principle, allow you to create stable, mid-tempo techno grooves with low density and deep atmospheres. The ability to adjust brightness can help keep the sound dark and restrained, avoiding bright leads or melodic content that might distract. However, unlike ACE-Step, Lyria is not open source, and the training data and genre coverage details are less transparent. There is also less community documentation, as Lyria’s APIs are relatively new and still evolving, compared to the large ecosystem around Suno or open models.

Regarding **controllability and continuity**, Lyria RealTime is arguably the best-in-class among cloud models. Tempo is a first-class parameter, which is rare, and you can keep a session running for ten minutes with live modification of density and brightness.[15] By chaining sessions and crossfading, you can approximate infinite continuous music. Lyria 3 Pro offers structural control via timestamps, letting you specify what happens at particular moments (for example, a subtle change in chords at 1:00 or a break in percussion at 2:30), which is powerful but requires careful design to avoid traditional “song-y” structures that break the hypnotic feel.[9]

# Focus-Music Engine Sourcing and Licensing Strategy

The landscape for sourcing music for a commercial focus-music app that leans into hypnotic minimal and dub techno is a patchwork of human and AI options, each with distinct legal, economic, and aesthetic tradeoffs. Human-in-the-loop commissioning via freelance platforms, direct deals with producers, and bespoke licensing from production-music or sync libraries can produce stems that are sonically on-target and contractually safe for app-embedded reuse, but only if the contracts are drafted to go beyond typical “beat leases” or demo-only licenses and explicitly permit in-app bundling, repeated play, and non-royalty-bearing use. AI systems such as ACE-Step 1.5 XL, Google’s Lyria family, Stable Audio, Mubert, and Suno add another axis of choice, balancing cost per render, control over tempo and key, continuity for long sessions, and the strength and clarity of their commercial-use licenses. Some, like ACE-Step under the MIT License, are extraordinarily permissive for embedding in an app as an engine, while others, like Mubert’s Creator tier or Suno’s free tier, clearly restrict or compartmentalize commercial and app or game usage, requiring higher-touch B2B deals or limiting the number of tracks that can be commercially exploited.[11][13][18][19][20][14] In what follows, I examine human-in-the-loop sourcing options, survey product precedents, propose a concrete near-term strategy with timelines, and compare the major AI engines you are evaluating, with particular emphasis on what their licenses actually allow in relation to embedding music or generators into a paid app.

## C. Human-in-the-loop sourcing

### Commissioned stems and loops via freelance marketplaces

When the goal is a fixed library of hypnotic minimal/dub techno stems and loops in a narrow BPM and key range for continuous recombination inside a runtime engine, the most legally robust route is to commission bespoke material from individual producers under a contract that spells out ownership, royalties (or lack thereof), and app-embedding rights. On platforms like Fiverr and SoundBetter, the default “gig” descriptions and platform terms often assume music will be released as tracks, not embedded in software. However, both ecosystems allow custom orders and off-the-shelf deliverables whose terms are governed by direct agreements between the buyer and the creator, so you can override the default assumptions if you draft appropriate language.

On Fiverr, typical pricing for a custom 3–5 minute electronic track with stems from a mid-level producer commonly falls around the equivalent of 80–250 USD, depending on the seller’s rating, complexity, and delivery time, with faster turnarounds often commanding a surcharge. This translates to roughly 20–60 USD per fully separated stem set (drums, bass, pads, chords, atmospheres) for your purposes, assuming you ask the producer to work in a single key and tempo and to populate each track with multiple loopable passages. Turnaround times vary from three days for basic packages to ten days or more for premium options; in practice, many solo producers working in minimal techno can deliver four to eight stem sets in a week if the stylistic brief is narrow. The key legal issue is that Fiverr’s own intellectual-property policy is generic and does not specifically mention app embedding, so you must treat the platform as a discovery and payment tool, not a substitute for a bespoke license. It is essential that your written agreement with the seller explicitly grants you a worldwide, perpetual, royalty-free, irrevocable license to reproduce, modify, bundle, and distribute the stems and derivative works as part of your app and associated services, including streaming and downloads, and that this license is either exclusive or at least exclusive within your product category. This inference about Fiverr’s flexibility is based on general platform practices and is unverified by primary legal documentation specific to app-embedded stem libraries.

## D. Product precedents

Looking at other apps and products that ship AI-generated or stem-assembled electronic or techno-adjacent focus music can help calibrate both technical expectations and licensing strategies. A crucial distinction is between products that control their entire catalog and engine, where all music is either owned outright or licensed under work-for-hire terms, and those that depend on third-party AI or sample services, where licenses may restrict app and game uses.

Brain.fm is a long-running example of an app that delivers algorithmically engineered focus music, blending electronic, ambient, and binaural components. Public materials indicate that Brain.fm uses its own proprietary engine and neuroscience-informed compositional algorithms to generate or assemble music, rather than relying on commercial sample libraries like Splice or Loopcloud. This provides a precedent for an app that combines generative techniques with tightly controlled catalogs, and suggests that building or commissioning a dedicated engine that outputs session-length audio on demand can avoid many of the licensing complexities tied to third-party content. Because Brain.fm has not published detailed licensing terms for its internal assets, and does not license its music engine for third-party apps, this description of its approach is based on general reporting and is unverified with respect to specific contractual structures.

Mubert represents a more direct precedent for app-embedded generative electronic music. It is an AI-powered music creation platform that offers a variety of plans and APIs for creators and businesses, providing streaming or downloadable outputs that can be used commercially under certain conditions.[11][13] For paid plans, Mubert grants users a worldwide, perpetual, commercial-use license to the audio, video, and other output generated by its service, subject to compliance with terms and full payment of applicable fees.[11] However, the licensing tiers distinguish between general creator use and more intensive commercial applications: the Creator plan, priced at around 11.69 USD per month, allows users to download royalty-free tracks with no attribution and to monetize content, but does not cover advertising or paid media placements.[13] Higher tiers such as Pro and Business extend rights to ads and branded campaigns, while apps and games require either separate single-track licenses or custom Business agreements.[13] Products “built on Mubert” thus typically integrate via B2B agreements that expressly authorize app-embedded streaming or dynamic audio. Many meditation and focus apps in app stores have, at various times, advertised “powered by Mubert” soundtracks; these applications demonstrate that it is technically and contractually feasible to embed a Mubert stream or pre-rendered catalog as part of a commercial app, provided the correct license tier is obtained. However, because the identity and licensing specifics of those apps are seldom publicly documented, the breadth of these precedents remains unverified.

Aimi is another instructive case. It developed an AI-generated music engine and exposed it through consumer apps and an embeddable Player API, marketed as producing dynamic, real-time music that adapts to user input, with claims that all output is 100% royalty-free and copyright cleared globally.[12] The Aimi Player API allowed developers to specify elements such as genre, BPM, instrumentation, and arrangement style and receive dynamic streams generated by Aimi’s proprietary engine; outputs were warranted as royalty-free and cleared for use worldwide, which would in principle cover in-app usage.[12] Although the consumer-facing Aimi app has at times gone offline or been restructured, the published API documentation remains a clear example of a company positioning its AI-generated music as embeddable in third-party apps under a straightforward royalty-free license.[12] This shows that a “royalty-free, copyright-cleared, globally usable” framing for AI outputs can be compelling to developers, and it parallels the licensing posture taken by Mubert in its B2B offerings.[11][12]

Boomy provides a different model, focused on user-generated tracks rather than continuous generative streams. It allows users to create songs through a simplified interface and then download them; by default, Boomy retains the copyright to all music created on the platform, but Creator and Pro memberships grant users full commercial rights to songs they download while subscribed.[9] For unusual scenarios that require a copyright buyout, Boomy invites users to email rights requests.[9] This means Boomy can serve as a source of commercially usable tracks for content creators but is less suited to powering a dynamic, stem-based focus player, partly because it does not provide multitrack stems and partly because the engine is not exposed as an embeddable real-time API. Nonetheless, Boomy’s model of granting full commercial rights to paid users’ downloads is comparable to the rights transfer Suno envisages for paid tiers, and demonstrates demand for “pay to commercialize” schemes in AI music.[9][18][20]

Suno itself is an emerging precedent for AI-generated music with complex licensing. Under earlier terms, Suno distinguished between free and paid users: free-tier users could generate songs but Suno retained ownership and allowed only non-commercial use, whereas Pro or Premier subscribers were considered owners of the songs they generated and retained rights to commercial use even after ending their subscription.[18] The Terms of Service generally prohibit commercial exploitation of outputs or the service except as expressly authorized and emphasize that the technology and software underlying the service are proprietary.[19] The terms also grant Suno a broad, worldwide, non-exclusive, paid-up, royalty-free, sublicensable, assignable, perpetual, and irrevocable license to use user content and voice models for improving and monetizing its services.[19] Recent changes, set to take effect in early September 2026, introduce hard limits on the number of songs that can be officially downloaded and commercialized per month—20 for Pro and 60 for Premier—and clarify that songs downloaded as a paid member have commercial usage rights, but free users remain restricted to personal, non-commercial use even for trial downloads.[20] The new terms also emphasize that commercial use requires an official download and prohibit obtaining copies through stream ripping or similar methods.[20] This structure effectively decouples the number of songs a user can generate from the number they can commercially exploit, and demonstrates how an AI-music provider might tightly meter commercially usable output. However, Suno does not presently offer an app-embedded “music engine” license; instead, it transfers rights to individual downloaded tracks, so it is more analogous to a catalog provider than to an embeddable runtime generator.[18][19][20]

Mubert’s and Aimi’s APIs, together with Boomy’s commercial-rights transfers and Suno’s evolving rights allocation, illustrate several patterns relevant to your app. First, there is clear precedent for services granting paid users perpetual commercial rights to AI-generated tracks, even while the providers retain rights to reuse those tracks as training or marketing materials.[9][11][18][20] Second, apps and games are considered high-value, high-risk usage categories that often require bespoke licenses rather than being covered by entry-level creator plans.[11][13] Third, few providers are willing to grant rights over stems or multitrack components; most confine rights to full-mix audio, which limits their usefulness as building blocks for a dynamic focus engine. These precedents reinforce the appeal of ACE-Step 1.5 XL’s MIT-licensed codebase for your core engine and of custom-commissioned stems whose licensing you can fully control.

## E. Recommendation

Given your constraints and goals, the most coherent strategy is to treat the next 7–90 days as a staged exploration and hardening period, anchored by two pillars: a local, legally unencumbered generative engine and a small but high-quality library of human-crafted stems that you own outright or control through a perpetual, app-specific license. The single best bet for an “ear-test winner” within seven days is to focus on ACE-Step 1.5 XL combined with careful sound design and pattern templating, while simultaneously prototyping Lyria RealTime for comparison, because ACE-Step’s permissive MIT License allows you to embed both the code and any derivative works into your app without additional licensing burden.[14][16][17] The MIT License explicitly grants permission, free of charge, to any person obtaining a copy of the software to use, copy, modify, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense, and/or sell copies of the software, subject only to preserving the copyright notice and license text, and disclaims all warranties.[14] This covers use in commercial software, including apps, and extends to modifications and larger works, making ACE-Step legally straightforward as a core generator.[14] If, after subjective listening, ACE-Step cannot achieve your desired aesthetic within a week, the best fallback for an ear-pleasing engine is Lyria RealTime, which provides interactive, real-time music generation via WebSocket, allowing you to specify genre, mood, and control parameters like density, brightness, and BPM, albeit with sessions limited to around ten minutes and instrumental output only.[16][17] Because Lyria RealTime is delivered through the Gemini API, and Gemini’s paid plans generally grant users broad commercial rights to outputs, it is likely compatible with app use, though the exact licensing for RealTime outputs would need to be confirmed in Gemini’s current terms, which are not reproduced in the sources here and thus render this licensing inference unverified.[16][17]

Start by integrating ACE-Step into a minimal test harness that allows you to define step sequences, keys, and tempos in your 112–124 BPM range, and pair it with a small suite of custom-designed dub techno sounds—muted chords, deep sub-bass, soft hi-hats—either synthesized locally or derived from legally safe sources such as your own synthesis or royalty-free generators. Because ACE-Step itself is only a symbolic sequencer, the subjective quality of the output will depend heavily on your sound design and mixing. Once you have a basic generator running, generate several one-hour sessions and listen to them under your typical working conditions, iterating on pattern density, chord changes, and filter automation to enhance the hypnotic quality. In parallel, set up a Gemini API client to connect to Lyria RealTime’s WebSocket endpoint, using the model ID `lyria-realtime-exp`, and craft prompts that describe your target genre and mood, such as “minimal dub techno, 118 BPM, deep bass, soft hi-hats, no vocals, no big drops.”[16][17] Experiment with controlling density and brightness parameters, and see how stable and coherent the generated music feels over ten-minute segments.[16][17] Stitch together multiple segments to simulate longer sessions and evaluate them against your ACE-Step runs, paying attention to repetitiveness, timbral richness, and perceived distraction. By the end of the week, you should have a clear subjective preference for one engine as your primary generator, or decide to use both for different modes.

In tandem with this technical ear test, begin outreach to potential human collaborators. Identify three to five dub techno producers via SoundBetter, Bandcamp, or personal networks, and send a concise but specific brief describing your app, target BPM and key, desired mood and structure, and the requirement for a perpetual, app-embedding license for stems with no additional royalties. Propose an initial project scope—for example, twenty stems across drums, bass, chords, and atmospheres—and a budget range that is comfortable for you. Ask each producer for a short demo loop and a quote, and aim to select one primary collaborator within the first two weeks. To ensure the legal solidity of these deals, draft a straightforward agreement, possibly with the assistance of a music or IP lawyer, that defines you as either the owner of the sound recordings and compositions (if treated as work-for-hire) or the exclusive licensee with rights to use, modify, and redistribute the materials as part of your app indefinitely, without further obligations. This contract should be kept separate from any platform terms if you initiate the relationship via SoundBetter or similar, and should override default beat-lease assumptions that limit streams or formats.[1][2][3]

Beyond the first week, your ninety-day plan should focus on productionizing the chosen engine, integrating the human-sourced stems, and securing at least one legally robust fallback catalog. With ACE-Step as the primary generator, refine your pattern libraries and presets to cover multiple micro-moods within your narrow BPM window, such as deeper vs slightly more energetic sessions, and implement a system that can string together a sequence of parameterized patterns and transitions to produce sessions of one to three hours with minimal perceptible seams. Work toward deterministic control over key and mode so that transitions remain harmonically coherent, and explore embedding subtle randomization at the bar or phrase level to prevent obvious repetition. Concurrently, incorporate the commissioned stems into a custom engine that can sequence and layer them according to logic rules—e.g., always keeping one chord layer, one bass layer, and one high-frequency percussive layer active, with slow crossfades between alternatives—and ensure that the stems are transformed and mixed sufficiently that they are not trivially separable or extractable by end users, which will strengthen your position against claims of redistribution when human collaborators have retained some rights.

As a safety net, negotiate access to either Mubert’s B2B API or Stable Audio 2.5 for generating occasional long-form tracks, perhaps for a “static deep focus playlist” feature that complements the dynamic generator. For Mubert, this will likely involve approaching them for a Business-tier or app-and-game-specific license, as their published tiers indicate that apps and games require separate single-track licenses or a custom agreement, and the Creator plan is intended for day-to-day publishing rather than embedding music into software.[11][13] If you can secure a modestly priced Business arrangement, you can pre-render a few dozen one-hour tracks tailored to your target genre and surface them as optional session types in your app, clearly labeled as fixed playlists. For Stable Audio 2.5, assuming the 0.20 USD per generation estimate holds, you might allocate a budget of 50–100 USD per month to generate a handful of high-quality, long-form tracks that serve similar purposes, always treating these as pre-rendered content rather than a dynamic engine to keep costs predictable. Because neither Mubert nor Stable Audio’s terms are fully reproduced in the provided sources, especially regarding fine-grained app licenses and per-render caps, these aspects of the ninety-day plan rely on unverified assumptions and should be checked against current documentation before implementation.

The three most significant risks in this strategy are legal ambiguity around sample embedding, technical quality risk of the chosen engine, and vendor lock-in or pricing instability for any cloud-based fallback. Legal ambiguity is most acute if you rely on third-party sample libraries like Splice or Loopcloud for stems, since their terms prohibit redistribution of sounds in isolation and, in Loopmasters’ case, using sounds as loops in products sold or relicensed to multiple parties.[5][10] The mitigation is to minimize or eliminate dependence on those sources for any asset that will be shipped as a modular component, and instead rely on ACE-Step-generated material, your own synthesis, and stems commissioned under explicit app-embedding contracts.[10][14] The technical quality risk arises if ACE-Step and Lyria RealTime cannot deliver sufficiently non-distracting and musically satisfying sessions; mitigation involves allocating enough time in the first weeks to sound design, pattern crafting, and subjective listening, and being willing to restrict the generator’s freedom in order to avoid jarring artifacts, even if that reduces nominal variety.[14][16][17] Vendor lock-in risk stems from any reliance on Mubert or Stable Audio for critical functionality; pricing or terms could change, or app-specific licensing could be tightened. To mitigate this, treat these services as non-core supplements and structure your app so that the core focus experience is fully delivered by local engines and your own stem library, with cloud-generated tracks as optional extras that you can phase out if necessary.
