## 4. Willingness‑to‑pay: who actually pays vs uses free options

You asked for concrete willingness‑to‑pay data specifically for focus music apps. Direct, app‑specific WTP surveys are scarce, but we can triangulate from broader **music streaming WTP data** and **wellness/focus app churn benchmarks**.

### 4a. Paid vs free music streaming — macro data

**US and global streaming behavior**

- A 2019–2025 comScore analysis found that in the U.S., roughly **three‑in‑four consumers (≈75%) use ad‑supported free versions** of online music streaming services rather than paying for ad‑free subscriptions.[3][4]  
  - **Free accounts comprise about 76% of total time spent** across streaming services.[3][4]  
  - However, **individual paid subscribers listen longer per person**, with ~628 monthly ad‑free minutes per paid user vs 583 ad‑supported minutes per free user.[3][4]
- A 2025 Edison Research update notes that, by time spent, **Americans 13+ now spend about 66% of their streaming‑music listening with paid platforms and 34% with free services**.[8] This indicates paid platforms have gained share of listening time, even though free users remain numerous.

**Global IFPI data (Engaging with Music reports)**

- IFPI’s 2023 “Engaging with Music” report finds **73% of people say they listen to music through licensed audio streaming services** (subscription and ad‑supported).[9]  
- Time spent on **subscription audio streaming** has increased, reflecting users’ desire for **ad‑free, on‑demand access to favorite songs and artists**.[9][15]

These are primary industry reports based on large surveys; they tell us **who pays for general music**, not focus‑only apps.

**Country‑specific WTP: India 2024–2026**

A large Indian study (EY + Indian Music Industry, 15k+ smartphone users) shows:

- In India, **paid subscribers of music streaming apps were only 10–11M in 2024** out of **175M active users** (≈6–7%).[6]  
- By 2026, paid subscriptions increased slightly to **14M**, but this is still only **about 7% of ~200M music‑app users**.[6]  
- **96%** of smartphone users surveyed listen to music on their phones; **80%** listen at least an hour per day.[6]  
- **Only 38% had ever paid for music**, and **non‑paying users often said they could not distinguish between free and paid services and felt free options were sufficient**.[6]  
- Importantly, **61% of non‑payers said they would be willing to pay if no free options were available and if price was reasonable**.[6]

This is a primary survey and gives direct insight into why people *don’t* pay: free options are “good enough” and differentiation is unclear.[6]

### 4b. Willingness to pay in freemium environments

An academic study on **willingness to pay for freemium music services** found:

- Using the free version of a music service has a **negative impact on users’ intention to pay** for the premium version.[13]  
- The authors recommend **time‑limited freemium** rather than indefinite free to mitigate this.[13]

This is a primary survey (157 participants) investigating attitudinal drivers in freemium contexts.[13]

### 4c. Who pays specifically for “focus” / functional music?

Direct data on “focus music app subscriptions” is limited, but we can infer from:

- A 2025 market report on **“Focus Music Apps”** states that:  
  - The **Freemium model is the dominant approach** for this category.[14]

**Implications**

- Paid streaming is growing, but **focus music is a sub‑category inside those services**, not necessarily a separate paid app for most people.

  - **Professionals** (knowledge workers, coders, writers) seeking deep‑work support.  
  - **Enterprise or team buyers** bundling focus tools for productivity.

Variety‑seeking focus listeners—who like switching playlists, channels, moods—face a very low marginal benefit from paying for a standalone focus app when **YouTube + Spotify + Mubert** already deliver huge variety for free or within their existing subscription.

## 5. Evidence on churn from focus/functional music apps

Direct churn data for “focus music apps” is sparse, but we have analogous evidence from **meditation/wellness apps** and freemium behaviors.

A 2026 **churn benchmark for meditation/wellness apps** (health & wellness category) identifies top self‑reported reasons for churn:

- **Practice inconsistency**: 41% of users stop using the app because they **stop meditating/stop the routine**.[7]  
- **Free alternatives**: 24% leave because of **free alternatives like YouTube or free app tiers**.[7]  
- **Perceived lack of progress/benefit**: 19% churn due to **not feeling improvement or meaningful benefit**.[7]

This is a primary survey/report of user churn reasons for wellness apps in general.[7]

While not music‑specific, the pattern is highly relevant:

- Focus music apps are also **habit‑dependent**: if a user stops structured deep‑work sessions or changes jobs, perceived need declines.
- Free alternatives (YouTube focus playlists, Spotify’s built‑in focus/sleep channels) are abundant; users quickly realize they can replicate the app’s function without paying.
- If users don’t feel **measurable cognitive benefit** (better concentration, productivity) beyond generic background music, they perceive the app as redundant.

Coupled with the freemium WTP findings, this suggests **high churn risk** for standalone focus‑music apps unless they create:

- Strong **perceived functional differentiation** (e.g., neuro‑scientific claims backed by studies, timers, task integration).
- Lock‑in via **workflows**, not just audio (e.g., Pomodoro timers, distraction blocking, analytics).

## Synthesis: Is the variety-seeking segment well-served, underserved, or unmonetizable?

Putting it all together:

1. **Supply & scale of free/cheap focus music**
   - **YouTube lofi streams** (especially Lofi Girl) already deliver **constant, ad‑supported non‑vocal focus audio** with **thousands of concurrent listeners, 15M+ subscribers, billions of views**, entirely free.[31][33][39][40]
   - **Spotify** offers **hundreds of branded and user‑curated focus/study/deep‑focus playlists**, with **tens of millions of cumulative followers**.[16][18][19][28] These are accessible to:
     - Free, ad‑supported Spotify users.  
     - Paid subscribers who get focus playlists as part of their broader music subscription.
   - **AI/stock music platforms (Mubert)** give infinite generative channels, with hundreds of thousands of installs and solid ratings, plus cheap premium tiers.[46][48][54][56][59]

   **Fact‑based conclusion**: The *variety* dimension—how many different non‑vocal focus streams a user can access—is already **richly served at very low or zero marginal cost**.

2. **User preferences & WTP**
   - Large‑scale surveys show **most users rely on free streaming tiers** when available and see them as sufficient.[3][4][6][13]  
   - Even in markets with fast streaming growth, paid penetration among music‑app users can be **single‑digit percentages**, and non‑payers explicitly say **“free options are sufficient”** and they **cannot distinguish** between free and paid services.[6]
   - Freemium research shows that **using the free tier reduces intention to upgrade**, pushing providers toward either **time‑limited freemium** or strong differentiation in premium features.[13]
   - Focus‑app market research indicates that **freemium dominates** and the **paid segment is smaller but more loyal and higher ARPU**, heavily skewed toward **professional and enterprise buyers**.[14]

   **Interpretation**: For **variety‑seeking general listeners**, the rational choice is to stay with free options (YouTube, ad‑supported Spotify, free Mubert channels) because **functional differences are minimal**; paying is rational only for **heavy, professional users** where deep work is mission‑critical.

3. **Churn & substitution**
   - Wellness app churn data shows **24% of users churn due to free alternatives** and **19% due to perceived lack of progress/benefit**.[7]
   - Focus apps share similar dynamics: usage drops when routines change and when users don’t perceive unique benefits vs generic music.

   **Implication**: Any standalone focus‑music app will see **substantial churn** among casual and variety‑seeking users, because the moment they re‑discover YouTube/Spotify focus playlists, the app looks redundant.

### Direct answer to your strategic question

- **Variety‑seeking focus music listeners *as a segment***:
  - **Well‑served**: On pure audio variety, they already have **massive free supply** and **integrated options within mainstream subscriptions** (Spotify, YouTube), plus AI‑generated streams (Mubert). There is no obvious *content scarcity* for non‑vocal focus music.
  - **Not fundamentally unservable**, but **hard to monetize**:
    - They can be monetized through **ads** (YouTube, free Spotify) and **bundled within general music subscriptions** (Spotify Premium, Apple Music, etc.).  
    - Creating a new **standalone paid focus‑music app aimed at variety‑seekers** faces a strong **“why pay?” barrier**, since the core value proposition (varied non‑vocal background music) looks functionally equivalent to free options.
  - **Underserved only in narrow niches**:
    - There may be underserved micro‑segments (e.g., users needing very specific non‑Western instruments, clinically validated soundscapes, or tightly coupled productivity workflows). These are small and likely overlap with **heavy‑user/professional segments** rather than casual variety‑seekers.

**Strategic implication**:  

A new paid focus‑music app **can win** some users, but *not* by targeting broad, variety‑seeking listeners on “better playlists” alone. That segment is **already well‑served and price‑anchored at zero**. Winning requires:

- Targeting **heavy/professional users** who care about **measurable deep‑focus outcomes** and are willing to pay for **tooling and rituals**, not just audio.
- Offering **clear, evidence‑backed differentiation** (e.g., cognitive‑science framing, integrations, analytics).
- Accepting that for **variety‑seekers**, the viable monetization levers are **ads, bundling**, or **freemium upsell for niche features**, not a simple paywall for access to music.
